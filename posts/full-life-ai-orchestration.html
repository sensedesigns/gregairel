The case for full-life AI orchestration · ai · agents

Greg AirelSeptember 17, 2026

Every idea and thought here is mine — AI helps maintain the grammar.

One place you send requests, in plain language. Behind it, a team of specialized agents that work both halves of your life — your inbox and your calendar, and also your thermostat, your kids' schedules, and the groceries.

Not one app per task. One point of contact that gets things done.

Where I actually am: the work side has been running for months and it works. The personal side is next, and I haven't built it yet. This post is both — what's live, and what I'm building toward.

Why I care about this

Time is the one thing you can't make more of, and most of us spend a shocking amount of ours on jobs that aren't hard, just numerous.

Count a normal week. At work: sorting email, scheduling meetings, writing the fourth version of the same reply. At home: building the grocery list and placing the order, fitting haircuts around soccer, reminding a kid about reading homework, working out what you owe the babysitter. None of it is difficult. Together it's hours.

The hours aren't the expensive part. The expensive part is the tracking. If you're a parent you know the feeling — you're sitting at dinner and some background process is still running on whether the registration renewal went out and what time practice starts Saturday. That noise never fully shuts off.

Getting the grocery hour back is nice. Handing off the tracking is the actual prize. The goal was never to do more. It's to be all the way present for the part that matters.

What it looks like

It's 6:45 a.m. Before you're out of bed, you send one message:

Give me a quick overview of the emails that came in overnight and flag anything that needs a reply before 10. For anything routine, draft responses based on previous threads and how I usually handle those emails, in my tone, and save them as drafts. Don't send anything. Tell me what meetings I have today, and pull the notes from last week's call with the Henderson account before my 11:00.

At 3:30, place the order for everything on our grocery list. At 7, remind Emma about her 30 minutes of reading.

Book haircuts for Jack and Lily Friday or Saturday afternoon — soccer is Saturday morning, so nothing before 1.

Set the thermostat to 70 overnight now that it's getting colder.

Pay the babysitter for this week. Read her texts to find the hours she worked and the rate she gave us, then send it to the Venmo account she gave us. Show me the total before it goes out.

Also: draft a reply to my manager proposing Thursday for the budget review, find a time next week for dinner with my parents and send my wife a couple of options, remind me to renew the car registration before it expires this month, and track the package that was supposed to arrive yesterday.

Two lines at 5 p.m.: what got done, what still needs me.

Then you get up and make coffee.

Read that list again and notice what it isn't. Nothing in it is clever. There's no task in there you couldn't do yourself in five minutes. That's exactly the point — it's fourteen five-minute jobs, which is most of a morning, and every one of them was sitting in your head before you sent the message.

What I'm running today

This isn't theoretical for me on the work side. My front door is Slack, with Claude and Cursor doing the orchestration underneath. I hand off a request in plain language, it gets broken into tasks, and the work comes back to me for review. Drafts land as drafts. Nothing goes out without my sign-off.

I haven't wired in my personal apps yet, and the design for that part is simple enough to say in a sentence. One orchestrator sits at the front door — Slack today, eventually a text or my voice. It decides whether a request is work or personal and routes it to the right chief of staff. Claude and Cursor keep the work side. Google Assistant is my plan for the personal side, because it already sits on top of my calendar, my phone, and the house. I talk to one thing; each side stays good at its own world.

The piece that makes it one system instead of two is shared memory. The soccer schedule on the personal side has to be able to inform when a work meeting gets booked. Without that you've just built two assistants who don't speak.

Personal Perspective

The surprise for me wasn't the time saved. It was how quickly I stopped writing careful prompts. Early on I wrote instructions like I was filing a ticket — precise, structured, defensive. Now I write the way I'd talk to someone who's worked for me for two years, because the memory layer means it mostly has. The setup work isn't building clever automations. It's front-loading context once so you never have to explain yourself again.

Where this is going

Most people using agents today point them at work: summarizing documents, triaging email, writing code. That's where the tools started, so that's where the integrations are.

But there's no clean line between work and life. The meeting that ran long is the reason nobody ordered groceries. The soccer schedule decides when the haircut can happen. Managing the two halves separately misses where the value actually is, which is in the seam between them.

The consumer side is closing fast. In August, xAI launched Grok Bot in beta — each bot gets its own cloud computer, signs into the apps you already use, and can act as a chief of staff managing specialist bots. Meta launched Muse, its own personal agent, this month. Open-source assistants like OpenClaw already let a technical user run a personal agent by texting it.

Being honest about the present: it's fragmented. You'll stitch several tools together, some integrations won't exist yet, and you'll need to be comfortable with a certain amount of setup that nobody has smoothed over. But it's possible today, and the pieces get simpler every month. My bet is that in five years this is as ordinary as a calendar app.

How to build it

Five layers. Each one does a single job and hands the request down.

1. The front door

  • What it is: the one place you send requests — Slack, a text, or your voice.
  • What it does: reads the message, splits it into tasks, routes each one to the work side or the personal side.
  • Mine: Slack.

2. Chiefs of staff

  • Work: plans and manages work tasks. Claude and Cursor.
  • Personal: plans and manages home and family. Google Assistant is the plan.
  • Both: schedule tasks for later ("at 3:30"), follow up, report back.

3. Agents

  • Email: triage the inbox, draft replies in your tone.
  • Meetings: manage the calendar, pull prep notes, schedule.
  • Home and family: thermostat, kids' reminders, appointments.
  • Errands: groceries, bookings, payments.

4. Tools and connectors

  • Work: Gmail, calendar, Slack, docs.
  • Personal: smart home, text messages, grocery apps, booking sites, Venmo.
  • Gaps: where no direct integration exists, a browser-using agent can usually fill in. Slower and more fragile — fine for a weekly errand, not for anything you need to be right every time.

5. Shared memory and approval rules

  • Memory: what every agent needs to know — names, schedules, preferences, your tone.
  • Approvals: what needs your yes first. Sending anything, spending anything, booking anything.
  • The rule: start strict. Loosen it as trust is earned, one category at a time.

Getting started

  1. Pick your front door. Whatever you already check all day. Don't add an app for this.
  2. Build the work arm first. Email and calendar integrations are the most mature by a wide margin, so it's where you'll get a win before you get frustrated.
  3. Add the personal arm one domain at a time. Reminders, then the smart home, then errands and payments. In that order — it's easiest to hardest, and it's also least to most expensive when something goes wrong.
  4. Keep drafts as drafts, and payments behind approval. Every time.
  5. Ask for a daily summary of everything done in your name. If you can't answer "what did it do today," you've given up the thing you were trying to buy.

Worth knowing before you start: the failure mode isn't an agent going rogue. It's an agent quietly doing nothing — a task that got scheduled and never ran, a draft that never got written, and you not finding out for three days because you'd stopped checking. That's what the daily summary is actually for. It's less a report than a heartbeat.

And a category to be deliberate about: anything involving your kids. I'll let an agent build a grocery list and set a thermostat without much thought. Reminders that go directly to a child, or bookings made in their name, get read by me first. Not because I expect a disaster — because the thing I'm buying here is presence, and outsourcing the last mile to my own kids would defeat the entire purpose.

The short version

Full-life AI orchestration isn't a future concept. It's a slightly messy present. The people setting it up now are doing what early smartphone adopters did — tolerating rough edges in exchange for a real change in how the day works.

The rough edges will smooth out. The payoff, which is time and mental space handed back to the people who deserve it, is already here.

I'll write up the personal arm once I've built it, including whatever breaks.
