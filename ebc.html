Everest Base Camp

A Personal Field Guide — ten days through the Khumbu, Lukla to Base Camp on foot and a helicopter out.

The full journey, shot on a GoPro — Lukla to Base Camp, and the flight home.

Everyone knows the destination. Almost nobody talks about the part that actually decides whether you reach it: going slowly enough.

This is the route as I walked it — a ten-day itinerary that trades one rest day in Namche Bazaar for a much better chance of enjoying the rest of the trip, and swaps the long walk back down the valley for a helicopter off the glacier. Below is what each day asks of you, and the numbers behind it.

Getting In

The trek starts with one of the more memorable flights in commercial aviation. Lukla's airstrip is cut into a hillside at 2,860 m, and it's the last place on the whole trip you'll see a wheel. From the plane you get around forty minutes of Himalaya, hill settlements and forest before the ground comes up to meet you.

Where you actually fly from

Worth knowing before you book: in the busy months — March, April, May, October and November — the Lukla flight usually departs from Ramechhap/Manthali rather than Kathmandu, because of air traffic. Manthali is about a four-hour drive away, which in practice means a 2:30 a.m. start and a shuttle before a twenty-minute flight. Outside those months you fly from Kathmandu itself, leaving around 5 or 6 a.m. for a thirty-five minute hop. The airlines make that call, not you.

From Lukla it's a short first day — around 8 km and three hours down to Phakding at 2,652 m, following the trail above the Dudh Koshi. You lose a little height, which is exactly right. Day two is the real start: 11 km up the valley through Benkar and Monjo, across suspension bridges over the Dudh Koshi and Bhote Koshi, then a steep climb into Namche Bazaar at 3,441 m. If the cloud cooperates, Everest and Lhotse appear about an hour before you get there.

The Day That Makes The Trek Work

Namche is the first serious altitude gain of the whole route, and it's where things go wrong for people in a hurry. So the itinerary spends a full day there without sleeping any higher: up to Syangboche and the Everest View Hotel, across into the Khumjung valley — a settlement over a thousand years old — and back down to the same bed.

Climb high, sleep low. It costs a day and it buys the remaining week. There is plenty to fill it with: the Sherpa Museum on Sherpa culture and mountaineering history, Khumjung's monastery and the scalp they say belonged to a yeti, or a walk out to Thame and the school Sir Edmund Hillary founded.

A planning decision worth making deliberately

Many versions of this trek build in a second rest day at Dingboche. This one doesn't, which shortens the trip but leaves less margin. If you've trained for a few months beforehand, one full day at Namche is often enough. If you'd rather have the buffer, add the Dingboche day back and make it eleven — or keep the ten and spend the spare day on the Kathmandu world heritage sites instead. Decide it in advance with whoever you're walking with, not at 4,300 m.

Up The Valley

Namche to Tengboche, 3,870 m. Around 10 km and five to six hours: down to the Dudh Koshi, then the well-known climb through rhododendron forest. Tengboche has the largest and most revered monastery in the region, and a skyline of Everest, Lhotse, Nuptse, Ama Dablam, Thamserku and Kongde.

Tengboche to Dingboche, 4,360 m. About 9 km and five hours through birch, rhododendron and conifer, crossing the Imja River near Pangboche. From here you start seeing stone-walled fields growing barley, buckwheat and potatoes at an altitude where none of that should be possible.

Dingboche to Lobuche, 4,940 m. Roughly 7 km and five hours, climbing gradually to Duglha at the end of the Khumbu Glacier's terminal moraine, then steeply up to Chupki Lhara — a ridge of memorial cairns and prayer flags for climbers who didn't come back, Scott Fischer and Babu Chiri Sherpa among them. It is a quiet stretch of trail, and it should be. Above it the route opens onto Lingtren, Pumori, Khumbutse and the Mahalangur Himal.

Base Camp Day

The long one: 13 km and seven to eight hours from Lobuche to Gorak Shep, out along the glacier to Everest Base Camp at 5,383 m, then back to Gorak Shep to sleep. Rocky dunes, moraine and meltwater, in air holding roughly half the oxygen you get at sea level. The route out is deceptive and the trail is not always obvious — this is a day to stay behind your guide.

Base Camp isn't a summit and doesn't pretend to be. In climbing season it's a scatter of bright expedition tents against grey rubble, with Nuptse, Khumbutse and Pumori standing over it. You spend a while, and then you walk back to the teahouse.

Kala Patthar, And The Way Out

The view everyone actually comes for costs a pre-dawn start. Kala Patthar is 5,555 m — 18,226 feet, the high point of the trip — a steep, uneven, breathless climb up to a viewpoint where the sun rises onto Everest with nothing in the way of it.

Then breakfast, and a helicopter off the glacier: over the valleys, rivers, forests and villages that took a week on foot, a refuelling stop at Lukla, and back into Kathmandu with the whole day still ahead of you. It is a strange and excellent way to end a trek — the descent you'd normally earn, handed to you in under an hour.

What It Takes

On paper this trek is moderate, and that's fair. The days are five to eight hours of walking on a well-established trail, with teahouses and hot meals at the end of them, porters carrying the heavy bags and a guide who has done it more times than you'll count. Nothing about the terrain is technical.

The difficulty is entirely the altitude, and it doesn't care how fit you are. Above 4,000 m the walking gets slower, sleep gets worse, and appetite disappears — which matters, because you still need to eat. Plan for cold at the top end, layer for a temperature range that spans a Kathmandu afternoon and a night at 5,000 m, and break your boots in long before you get on a plane. Any decent operator will hand you a full kit list; the parts worth spending real money on are boots, an insulated jacket and a sleeping system.

Two months of consistent uphill training beforehand does more for your chances than any piece of equipment.
